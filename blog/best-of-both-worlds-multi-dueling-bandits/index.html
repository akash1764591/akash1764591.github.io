Best-of-Both-Worlds Multi-Dueling Bandits

S Akash · August 10, 2026

During my bachelor’s thesis, I worked with Pratik Gajane and Jawar Singh on multi-dueling bandits. The question was whether a learner that sees only the winner of each comparison can exploit stable preferences without becoming fragile when those preferences change adversarially.

Winner-only feedback

Suppose I want to compare several search rankers. If I show a user two rankings and record which one they prefer, that is a dueling bandit. A multi-dueling bandit lets me show \(m \ge 2\) rankers at once, but I still get only one winner back.

Here is the model we use. There are \(K\) arms. At round \(t\), the learner chooses a multiset \(A_t\) of size \(m\), then observes the winning position \(I_t\), whose arm is \(A_t(I_t)\). Pairwise preferences live in a matrix \(P_t\), where \(P_t(i,j)\) is the probability that \(i\) is preferred to \(j\):

\[ P_t(i,j) + P_t(j,i) = 1, \qquad P_t(i,i) = \tfrac12. \]

We still need a rule that turns this pairwise matrix into one winner among \(m\) arms. Under the pairwise-subset choice model, position \(i\) wins with its average pairwise preference against the other positions:

\[ W(i\mid A,P) =\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{2P(A(i),A(j))}{m(m-1)}. \]

What can change over time?

In a stochastic environment, \(P_t=P\): evidence collected today remains useful tomorrow. In an adversarial environment, \(P_t\) may change every round. Commit early and later changes can trap you; keep exploring forever and you waste an easy, stable problem.

The learner is not told which world it is in. This is the best-of-both-worlds goal: benefit from stability without assuming it.

What does “best” mean?

Pairwise preferences need not fit a single ranking: \(A\) can beat \(B\), \(B\) can beat \(C\), and \(C\) can beat \(A\). So “best” needs a definition.

We use two. A Condorcet winner \(a^\star\) beats every opponent directly,

\[ P_t(a^\star,i) \ge \tfrac12 \qquad \text{for every arm } i \text{ and round } t, \]

while a Borda winner maximizes the average preference against all opponents,

\[ b_t(i)=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\ne i}P_t(i,j). \]

The two winners can disagree. Consider three arms with

\[ P(A,B)=0.51,\qquad P(A,C)=0.51,\qquad P(B,C)=0.99. \]

Arm \(A\) is the Condorcet winner because it beats both opponents. But \(b(A)=0.51\) while \(b(B)=0.74\), so \(B\) is the Borda winner. Preserving head-to-head preferences is enough for Condorcet; Borda requires information from the whole matrix.

MetaDueling: reuse a dueling learner

For Condorcet, we take an ordinary dueling learner \(\mathcal D\) and put it behind the multi-dueling interface. The wrapper is MetaDueling:

Algorithm 1 MetaDueling
\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Require: }\text{dueling learner }\mathcal D,\ \text{subset size }m \\[2pt] &\textbf{for }t=1,\ldots,T\textbf{ do}\\ &\qquad (x_t,y_t)\leftarrow \mathcal D\\ &\qquad n_x+n_y=m,\quad |n_x-n_y|\le 1\\ &\qquad \text{if }m\text{ is odd, randomize which arm receives the extra copy}\\ &\qquad A_t\leftarrow \{\underbrace{x_t,\ldots,x_t}_{n_x}, \underbrace{y_t,\ldots,y_t}_{n_y}\}\\ &\qquad \text{play }A_t\text{ and observe winning position }I_t\\ &\qquad o_t\leftarrow\mathbb{1}\{A_t(I_t)=x_t\}\\ &\qquad \text{update }\mathcal D\text{ with }o_t\\ &\textbf{end for} \end{aligned} \]

At first this looks wasteful: a four-way comparison becomes \(A_t=\{x_t,x_t,y_t,y_t\}\). Some of the implicit comparisons are now \(x_t\) against itself or \(y_t\) against itself. But the \(m=4\) calculation shows exactly what the learner receives. If \(p=P_t(x_t,y_t)\), then

\[ \Pr(x_t\text{ wins}) =\frac16+\frac23p, \qquad \Pr(x_t\text{ wins})-\frac12 =\frac23\left(p-\frac12\right). \]

So a \(70\)-\(30\) duel looks roughly \(63\)-\(37\) to \(\mathcal D\).

The equation behind the reduction

For any \(m\), the same pattern gives the effective preference seen by the dueling learner:

\[ \mathbb E[o_t\mid x_t,y_t] = \alpha_m+\beta_m p =:\widetilde P_t(x_t,y_t), \] \[ \beta_m= \begin{cases} \dfrac{m}{2(m-1)}, & m \text{ even},\\[5pt] \dfrac{m+1}{2m}, & m \text{ odd}, \end{cases} \qquad \alpha_m=\frac{1-\beta_m}{2}. \]

Subtracting \(1/2\) from both sides makes the point visible:

\[ \widetilde P_t(x,y)-\tfrac12 =\beta_m\!\left(P_t(x,y)-\tfrac12\right) \]

The dueling learner sees the same contest, with every margin shrunk by \(\beta_m\):

  • The winner of every pair stays the same, so the Condorcet winner stays the same.
  • All Condorcet gaps shrink by the same factor.
  • Since \(\beta_m\ge \tfrac12\), the signal is never less than half as strong, no matter how large \(m\) is.

So \(\mathcal D\) can behave as though it were facing an ordinary duel. In our paper we use Versatile-DB; its ability to adapt between stochastic and adversarial duels passes through the reduction. MetaDueling only handles the translation.

SA-MiDEX: learn, monitor, switch

The same shortcut is not enough for Borda. For the adversarial case, we start from Pratik Gajane’s MiDEX (Multi Dueling EXP3). MiDEX keeps an EXP3 distribution \(q_t\) over the arms. Each round, it draws two arms from \(q_t\), passes them through the duplication-and-debiasing step above, and uses the result to update \(q_t\).

There is one catch: \(q_t\) favors arms that currently look good, while Borda averages against a uniform opponent. Importance weighting corrects that mismatch. The estimator is nonzero only for the sampled arms, but averaged over the learner’s random draws, it ranks the arms exactly as Borda does.

MiDEX handles changing preferences by never stopping exploration. SA-MiDEX keeps this adversarial mode as a fallback and adds a stochastic front end based on the strategy of Auer and Chiang.

SA-MiDEX begins by comparing every ordered pair to build a baseline matrix. In the stochastic phase, confidence intervals eliminate arms whose Borda scores are clearly worse, while occasional random pairs check whether the baseline still fits.

MetaDueling returns the effective preference \(\widetilde P_t\), not the underlying \(P_t\). SA-MiDEX undoes that affine map:

\[ g_t=\frac{o_t-\alpha_m}{\beta_m}, \qquad \mathbb E[g_t\mid x_t,y_t]=P_t(x_t,y_t). \]

Undoing the compression makes the sample noisier. During the stochastic phase, SA-MiDEX pairs a candidate \(x_t\) with an opponent \(Y_t\) chosen uniformly from every other arm. Averaging over that opponent gives exactly the Borda score:

\[ \mathbb E[g_t\mid x_t=x] =\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{y\ne x}P_t(x,y) =b_t(x). \]

At the same time, the monitor accumulates the difference between this feedback and the baseline prediction. Random noise grows on a square-root scale; a sustained shift grows faster. Once the discrepancy crosses the threshold, the stochastic phase ends and MiDEX takes over permanently.

What remains open

The questions I find most interesting are whether genuinely multi-way comparisons can be used without losing the black-box reduction, whether structure in the preference matrix can avoid all-pairs exploration, and whether the monitoring test can be made reversible. Both algorithms also rely on the pairwise-subset choice model; other choice models may need different ideas.
